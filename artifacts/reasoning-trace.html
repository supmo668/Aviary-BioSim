How the agent chose H1

An agent proposed three ways to raise recombinant proinsulin yield and took one forward. Because every stage was traced, we can read back what it discarded and on what grounds — and check whether the grounds were good ones.

The hypothesis space

Three candidates entered. The reasons two were cut are the interpretable part.

H1taken forward

PDI1 co-expression raises soluble, correctly folded proinsulin ≥ 2-fold.

Kept because: it tests the most mechanistically relevant limitation — ER oxidoreductase capacity — with a single genetic modification, and a negative result stays interpretable: either proinsulin is not PDI-limited, or excess PDI retains misfolded protein.

H2rejected

Co-overexpressing PDI1 with Ero1 beats PDI1 alone.

Cut because: it adds a second variable and higher cost, and a negative result would be confounded by Ero1-induced oxidative stress and UPR activation. Deferred to a follow-up conditional on H1 showing a PDI-dependent effect.

H3rejected

Lowering induction temperature to 20–25 °C raises folded yield.

Cut because: it tests a generic kinetic effect rather than the specific disulfide-folding limitation. A negative result could simply reflect lower specific productivity — ruling nothing out about oxidoreductase capacity.

Both rejections turn on the same criterion — and it is not which hypothesis is most likely to be true. It is which one yields an interpretable negative result.

The selection rule, recovered from the trace rather than specified in the prompt

The prompt asked the model to weigh prior evidence, cost to test, and how interpretable a negative result would be. It weighted the third above the first — H3 was acknowledged as cheap and still cut, because a null from H3 would be uninformative. That is a defensible experimental-design instinct, and it is visible only because the rejection reasons were captured alongside the choice.

Two findings about the instrumentation itself

Instrumented ≠ readable

The build loop that produced this system emits a span per iteration and publishes them successfully — yet querying its traces returns zero.

weave.publish() writes an object, not a call. Different store, invisible to the trace query. Six closes also landed as six versions of one object, not six objects.

This pipeline uses @weave.op instead, so its stages are real calls and queryable. A system can be perfectly instrumented and unreadable by its own documented procedure.

The budget hid the answer

The first two runs returned empty content with finish_reason: length. Nothing was wrong with the prompt — reasoning tokens are billed against max_tokens, so a ceiling sized for the answer is consumed entirely by the thinking.

Fixed by escalating the budget on empty content rather than guessing a number. The grounding stage needed 6,692 tokens to emit 3,072 characters.

What this does and does not show

Read this before trusting the above

  • The trace shows what the model said its reasons were, not what caused the output. Stated rationale and actual mechanism are different things, and only the first is captured here.
  • Three hypotheses were requested, so three appeared. The trace cannot show what a broader or narrower request would have surfaced — the hypothesis space was bounded by the prompt.
  • Chars-per-token is a proxy for deliberation, not a measure of it. It separates reasoning-heavy from transcription-heavy stages; it says nothing about whether the reasoning was any good.
  • No experiment has been run. The design's quality is assessable from the literature; its conclusions are not, because there are none.
  • Citations were checked to correspond to real published work. That check was manual, not part of the traced pipeline.

The honest claim is narrow and still worth something: for this run, the decision structure is recoverable. You can see which alternatives existed, which were discarded, on what stated grounds, and how much deliberation each step consumed — without re-running anything.
